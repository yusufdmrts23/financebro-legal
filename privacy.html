8. Analytics & identifiers

We use first-party analytics (PostHog) and crash reporting (Sentry) that rely on device and usage identifiers to measure how the App is used and to fix problems. Those two are used for our own purposes only, and we do not send anything in them to an advertiser. From version 2.7, PostHog also records, once, whether you tapped Allow on Apple's tracking prompt, so we know what share of our advertising measurement includes Apple's advertising identifier; we do not send that record to Meta. Your answer is not kept only there: Meta's software reports it with its events (section 2), and RevenueCat's software keeps it on your subscription record (see Apple Ads below).

Separately, since version 2.6, Meta's measurement software sends the install, app-open and purchase events and the three one-time milestones described in section 2, while Advertising measurement is switched on, so we can tell whether our advertisements worked. It creates its own identifier for this App on your device. In version 2.6 it is not given Apple's advertising identifier (IDFA): that version presents no App Tracking Transparency prompt, and it switches off the identifier collection in Meta's software as well, so there are two independent reasons the identifier never leaves your device. From version 2.7 the App presents Apple's prompt and switches that collection on only once you tap Allow (and off again if you later turn tracking off for the App, or switch Advertising measurement off), so Meta's software can attach the IDFA to its events only for anyone who taps Allow. For anyone who taps Ask App Not to Track, both reasons still hold: iOS does not give the App the IDFA, and the collection stays off, so the events go as in 2.6, without the identifier (section 4, Apple's tracking prompt). The events are sent from your device one by one, each with the identifier Meta's software creates for this App, your IP address and basic device information; Meta reports results back to us only in aggregate. Unless you tap Allow on Apple's prompt, we do not give Meta the identifier that is normally used to join activity across companies' apps. Meta still receives your IP address, device details and, for purchases, Apple's transaction identifiers, so what it may do with these events is governed by its own terms and Apple's rules, not by anything that makes linking them technically impossible.

From version 2.7, Apple's App Store privacy label for this App declares Data Used to Track You. It says so because, for anyone who taps Allow on Apple's tracking prompt, Meta's software can attach Apple's advertising identifier to the events described in section 2, and Meta's terms allow it to use those events to target and deliver ads (section 4), even though we do not ourselves link data from the App with other companies' data. Through version 2.6 the label declared no tracking: that version never asks for tracking permission and never gives Meta Apple's advertising identifier or any account details, and we do not ourselves link data from the App with other companies' data. Apple's definition of tracking also covers a third-party SDK that combines an app's data with data from other companies' apps for advertising, even if the app's developer does not use it that way. The 2.6 label listed the data these events contain — a device identifier, product interaction and purchase history — under the purpose Third-Party Advertising.

Apple Ads. If you found Financebro through an ad in the App Store itself, Apple's attribution service (AdServices) can tell us so. On every install, in every region, the software from our subscription provider RevenueCat that is built into the App asks Apple, on your device, for an attribution token, and sends it to RevenueCat together with your account identifier. It does not use Apple's advertising identifier and does not depend on your answer to Apple's tracking prompt, and nothing about this is shared with Meta. Separately, RevenueCat's software also stores, by default, the App's tracking-permission status on your subscription record: it reads the status from your iPhone, sends it with each purchase or restore it reports, and updates it when the status changes. Since version 2.6 the App discloses the advertising-measurement events described in section 2 — app install, app opens, subscription purchases with their price and Apple's transaction identifiers, and three one-time milestones — to Meta so we can measure whether our own advertisements worked. It is switched on by default. Meta may use those events for its own purposes under its Business Tools Terms, which is why we treat this as sharing and offer an opt-out, even though we do not build advertising profiles. California also defines “sale” broadly; to the extent this could be treated as a sale, the same opt-out covers that too. In version 2.6 Meta is not given Apple's advertising identifier. From version 2.7 the events can carry it for anyone who taps Allow on Apple's tracking prompt (section 4); for anyone who taps Ask App Not to Track, they are shared without it, as in 2.6. Two things limit it. First, we turn on Meta's Limited Data Use setting and let Meta work out where you are: if Meta places you in a US state where it offers that setting, Meta says it handles these events as our service provider or processor and limits its own use of them. As of September 2026 those states are California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Maryland, Minnesota, Montana, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Oregon, Rhode Island and Texas (Meta keeps the current list here); it does not apply anywhere else, including Brazil. Second, you can stop it at Profile → Settings → Advertising measurement. Once that is off on a device, the App sends nothing further to Meta from it, apart from what Meta's software may still send until the App is fully closed, and Apple's SKAdNetwork report, both described in section 4.
